Cholesterol Markers, Explained

A standard cholesterol test tells you how much cholesterol is in your blood. The newer tests tell you how many particles are carrying it. That second number is the one that predicts plaque best. Here is what each test means, how it is ordered, and what it costs.

The Big Idea

Think of Your Artery as a Highway

Passengers and vehicles

Cholesterol is the passengers. Particles are the vehicles. Each bad vehicle has a patch of sticky black tar on the outside, called ApoB. Vehicles hit the artery wall. Passengers do not.

Person A: a few big buses

12 passengers (LDL cholesterol)
3 vehicles, 3 tar patches

Person B: lots of small cars

12 passengers (LDL cholesterol)
12 vehicles, 12 tar patches
Same LDL cholesterol on a standard test. Four times the traffic for Person B.

More vehicles means smaller vehicles, and the small ones lodge in the artery wall like BBs. The big ones are fluffy clouds that bounce off. Only a particle test (ApoB or the NMR) shows the difference. This pattern is most common with high triglycerides, belly weight, or blood sugar trouble.

The Names, Decoded

Big B, Big A, Little a

"Apo" is short for apolipoprotein. It is a protein on the outside of each particle, not the cholesterol inside.

ApoB

Say "A-po B" (big B)

The sticky black tar on every plaque-building vehicle. It is why a car sticks in the artery wall and dumps its passengers. Blood sugar, weight, and diet put more tarred cars on the road. Lower is better.

ApoA-1

Say "A-po A one" (big A)

The tow hook on the outside of the tow trucks (HDL). They haul cholesterol off the road and back to the liver. Higher is generally better.

Lp(a)

Say "L-P little a"

A car that leaves the factory with a double coat of tar. Extra sticky, and it collects clots. The factory is your genes, not your diet. Lower is better.

Easy way to remember: big B is the Bad tar you can change, big A is the tow hook, little a is the extra tar you inherit.

A Little History

"Normal" Cholesterol Keeps Moving

What counts as an acceptable number has dropped a lot in one lifetime. A result your parents were told was fine would get a very different conversation today.

WhenWhat was considered acceptable
1963A leading medical journal's table of normal lab values listed total cholesterol of 150 to 280 as normal.
1983In a national survey, the typical doctor did not start cholesterol medication until total cholesterol reached 340 to 359.
1986That typical starting point had dropped to 300 to 319.
1988The first national guideline: total cholesterol under 200 is desirable, 240 and above is high.
1990The typical starting point for medication was down to 240 to 259.
2001The national guideline named an LDL under 100 "optimal."
2026The current guideline sets LDL goals of under 100, under 70, or under 55, depending on your overall risk. It also says every adult should have Lp(a) checked once.
The cholesterol itself did not change. What changed is how early we try to catch the problem, and which markers we look at to find it.
